# Is Your Business Shit?

## When polishing creates value, and when you're just making shit shinier

There is an old saying in business:

**You can't polish a turd.**

The meaning is obvious enough. If the underlying product is bad, better branding, a prettier website, additional features, expensive marketing, and endless refinement will not magically make it valuable.

Usually, that is true.

Unfortunately for the metaphor, Newfoundland has moose-poop jewelry.

Literal shit.

Polished, preserved, packaged, turned into earrings and souvenirs, and sold to tourists.

I know because I have bought it myself as souvenirs for friends.

So apparently you *can* polish a turd.

More importantly, sometimes people will pay you for it.

That gives us a much more useful business question:

> **Why can a business selling literal shit outperform a metaphorically shit business that has spent months or years being polished?**

The answer has very little to do with polish.

It has everything to do with understanding where the value actually lives.

---

# Sometimes Shit Is the Product

Nobody buying moose-poop jewelry believes they are purchasing fine gemstones.

That would be a considerably different customer complaint.

The customer knows exactly what it is.

In fact, **what it is is the entire reason it works.**

Newfoundland has always had a particular affection for quirky, funny, unmistakably local things. Something can be slightly ridiculous and become more appealing because of it.

Moose-poop jewelry fits beautifully into that.

When I bought it for friends, I was technically purchasing processed moose shit.

But that was not really what I was buying.

I was buying a Newfoundland souvenir.

I was buying a joke.

I was buying something unusual to bring home.

I was buying the eventual reaction when somebody opened it and discovered what the earrings were made from.

I was buying a story.

The physical material may have almost no conventional value.

The **context of the material** creates the value.

And that distinction matters far beyond moose poop.

---

# What Did You Build Versus What Did They Buy?

Businesses naturally describe products according to what they created.

The customer often describes the same product completely differently.

The maker of moose-poop earrings might see sourcing, preservation, resin, jewelry hardware, packaging, labour, pricing and distribution.

The tourist sees:

**"Holy shit. These are made from actual moose shit."**

Sale.

That difference is a Perspective Gap.

And it exists everywhere.

A restaurant thinks it sells food.

One customer may actually be buying convenience.

Another may be buying atmosphere.

Another may be buying somewhere they can bring their children without feeling stressed.

Another may be buying a place to sit for two hours with a friend.

A software company thinks it sells a sophisticated platform.

Its customer may be buying five fewer hours of repetitive work every week.

A tourism operator thinks it sells admission to an attraction.

The tourist may be buying the photograph, the story, the memory, or something recognizably local to take home.

So one of the most useful questions a business can ask is not:

**What did we build?**

It is:

> **What does the customer believe they are buying?**

Those answers can be wildly different.

---

# The Metaphorically Shit Business

Now consider another business.

The product launches.

People are not particularly interested.

So the team improves the website.

Still not much interest.

They add features.

Then integrations.

Then customization.

The interface gets redesigned.

The graphics improve.

The animations become smoother.

More options are added.

The product becomes increasingly sophisticated.

Months of development may pass.

Everyone is working.

Everyone is busy.

There are always new screenshots, new features, new versions and new things to announce.

That creates a dangerous illusion:

> **Activity begins to masquerade as progress.**

Something is unquestionably being improved.

But nobody has stopped to ask whether the improvement is occurring in a place customers actually value.

This is how a business can eventually create a spectacularly polished solution to a problem nobody particularly wanted solved.

The polish is real.

The work is real.

The technical accomplishment may be real.

The demand still isn't.

---

# Literal Shit Versus Metaphorical Shit

This is where the business selling literal shit suddenly becomes rather impressive.

Moose-poop jewelry has a recognizable reason to exist.

It has novelty.

It has humour.

It has regional identity.

It has tourism value.

It creates a reaction.

It gives the buyer a story.

Its marketability is partially embedded in the product itself.

Compare that with a metaphorically bad product that needs increasingly elaborate explanations, features and marketing simply to convince people to notice it.

The literal shit may actually have the stronger business.

That does not mean moose-poop earrings will remain equally desirable forever.

Novelty can fade.

Generations change.

Tourist tastes change.

Something that delighted visitors twenty years ago may get a considerably weaker reaction from younger customers.

The cultural joke may become familiar.

Other novelty products may replace it.

That is normal.

But there is an important difference between:

**a product whose value is declining**

and

**a product that never had a compelling source of value in the first place.**

One may need to evolve.

The other may need to be reconsidered entirely.

---

# Polish Cannot Create the Reason to Care

Polish has value.

Good design matters.

Usability matters.

Presentation matters.

Packaging matters.

A physical environment matters.

Customer experience matters.

Technical quality matters.

The mistake is treating polish as though it has independent value.

A better way to think about it is:

> **Polish is a multiplier.**

If customers already have a reason to care, polish can make the experience substantially better.

If the product is useful, better usability helps.

If the product is desirable, better presentation helps.

If the product has a great story, good branding helps communicate it.

If the product has novelty, good packaging can amplify the novelty.

But if the fundamental reason to care is effectively zero:

**0 × beautiful = 0**

You can make zero gorgeous.

It remains zero.

This is the distinction many businesses miss.

They are not polishing something customers already value.

They are hoping enough polish will eventually **create the value itself.**

---

# This Happens Offline Too

Technology makes this behaviour particularly easy to see.

Software can accumulate features almost indefinitely. Websites can be redesigned repeatedly. New integrations can always be built. Interfaces can always become more elaborate.

For that reason, many Northframe case studies naturally involve online businesses, digital products and technology.

But this is not an online-business problem.

A brick-and-mortar business can do exactly the same thing.

A restaurant can renovate while ignoring why customers are not returning.

A retail store can redesign its displays while stocking products the local market does not want.

A café can buy better furniture while the problem is service.

A tourism business can improve its facilities while misunderstanding what visitors actually came to experience.

A shop can completely modernize itself and accidentally remove the quirky characteristic customers loved.

The physical or digital environment changes.

The diagnostic does not.

> **Are you improving something customers already value, or are you improving it because you hope the improvement will make them value it?**

Those are not the same thing.

---

# Sometimes the "Problem" Is the Product

Traditional improvement tends to assume that weaknesses should be removed.

Usually that is sensible.

But sometimes the supposed weakness is where the marketability lives.

Moose poop is an objectively terrible material if your goal is to compete with conventional fine jewelry.

Fortunately, that is not the competition.

The fact that the material is absurd is what makes it interesting.

Removing the absurdity would remove the product.

This happens in less ridiculous ways all the time.

Something a founder considers unsophisticated may be exactly what customers find approachable.

A strange design may be what makes something recognizable.

A limitation may make a product easier to understand.

A local characteristic may be more commercially valuable than trying to appear global.

A feature the company barely considered important may turn out to be the reason customers arrived.

This is why businesses need perspectives outside the people who built them.

The creator knows what was difficult to create.

The customer only knows what matters to them.

Those are not automatically the same thing.

---

# Built-In Marketability

There is another reason the moose-poop example works.

It contains part of its own marketing.

Imagine somebody asking:

**"What did you bring home from Newfoundland?"**

"A pair of earrings."

Not especially interesting.

Now:

**"A pair of earrings made from moose poop."**

You have a conversation.

That reaction has marketing value.

A product does not need to be outrageous to have built-in marketability.

It might have an immediately understandable result.

A striking design.

A compelling transformation.

A highly specific use.

An unusual origin.

A strong identity.

A surprising feature.

A story people naturally repeat.

The important part is that the marketing team is **revealing or amplifying something interesting that already exists.**

Otherwise marketing gets saddled with a much harder job:

manufacturing a reason to care around something that does not naturally provide one.

That leads to another useful diagnostic question:

> **Are you marketing the product, or are you constantly trying to manufacture a reason for the product to be interesting?**

---

# The Overpolishing Problem

Once the central value exists, refinement makes sense.

But even good products can be overpolished.

Eventually another hour, week or month of work produces almost no additional customer value.

Internally, the improvement may still feel significant because the team can see every detail.

Externally, the customer may barely notice.

This is where sunk effort, pride and perspective can become dangerous.

The organization continues polishing because polishing is measurable.

Testing whether the market actually cares is considerably less comfortable.

There is always something else that can be perfected.

The better question becomes:

> **What would happen if we stopped polishing this today?**

Would customers notice?

Would conversion change?

Would retention change?

Would the product become less useful?

Would anybody care?

If the answer is no, the next layer of polish may not be product development at all.

It may simply be motion.

---

# The Shit Business Test

So, is your business shit?

Maybe.

But that is not actually enough information to diagnose it.

A better investigation asks:

**What are customers truly buying?**

**Where does the value live?**

**Do customers recognize that value quickly?**

**Is your supposed weakness actually part of the appeal?**

**Does the product contain anything naturally worth talking about?**

**Is additional polish amplifying demand, or compensating for its absence?**

**If you stripped away the presentation, would there still be a compelling reason for the product to exist?**

And perhaps most importantly:

> **Are customers asking for something better, or are you repeatedly making it better because they never asked for it at all?**

---

# A Literal Shit Business Can Still Beat Yours

Moose-poop jewelry may not retain exactly the same novelty forever.

New generations may appreciate it less.

Tourism changes.

Humour changes.

Markets change.

The product may eventually need to evolve.

But at least, at some point, somebody understood exactly why another human being might exchange money for polished moose shit.

That is more market understanding than some considerably more sophisticated businesses ever achieve.

One business starts with something ridiculous and discovers where its value lives.

Another builds something nobody particularly wants and spends years making it prettier.

The first business may eventually need adaptation.

The second cannot polish its way out of the original problem.

So yes:

**You can polish shit and still have shit.**

**Sometimes polished shit sells extremely well.**

And occasionally, a business selling literal shit will outperform your metaphorically shit business because it understood something you didn't:

> **The polish was never the value.**

The value was knowing **why somebody wanted the shit in the first place.**
